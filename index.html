Prediction, evidence and uncertainty

The goal: predict uncertain outcomes, calibrate our confidence, and improve our estimates with additional evidence.

Horse racing is an exceptional playground: abundant public data and frequent, clear outcomes allow quick development and evaluation.

Market odds give us probabilities to compare against. We build our own forecasts, add researched evidence, and investigate which disagreements deserve attention.

Start here / Odds, probabilities and uncertainty

The odds are a forecast.
How do we test one?

1. Read a price as a probability.

Odds describe a payout. We use decimal odds: 2.00 means a total return of two units for each unit staked if the horse wins, including the original unit. Taking the reciprocal gives the raw implied probability: 1 / 2.00 = 50%. Shorter odds imply a higher probability; longer odds imply a lower one. [1]

A field is the set of horses in a race. A bookmaker can offer decimal odds of 2.00, 3.00 and 4.00 for its three runners. Their reciprocals are 50%, 33.33% and 25%: a total of 108.33%. Those offered prices do not have to normalize to 100%; bookmakers typically build an excess into their prices. Odds are prices from which we derive implied probabilities, rather than a ready-made probability distribution.

Imagine a race with three horses: Prior Belief, Bayes Factor and Posterior Favourite. We will follow these invented runners through the examples below.

The excess above 100% is called the overround. Here it is 8.33 percentage points. Dividing each raw probability by their total is one simple way to make a comparable distribution. This removes the excess proportionally; it does not recover the true winning probabilities or prove the market is calibrated.

We treat a relatively efficient, competitive market as a strong reference, while recognizing known biases. That is a starting premise, not a measured efficiency result for this dataset. [2] The market has read the form book too.

2. Understand what a 20% forecast commits us to.

A model assigning a 20% probability of winning expresses an estimate conditional on the information available before the race. The outcome, though, will be binary: the horse either wins or does not. The probability must then be interpreted as uncertainty about that outcome.

Calibration is the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies. If events assigned approximately 20% consistently occur at a rate closer to 40%, the model underestimates their likelihood. An individual outcome cannot establish such a discrepancy. This allows probabilities for unique events to be assessed through a forecaster’s broader record. [3] This means that, statistically, if a well-calibrated model assigns a 20% probability to a collection of events, approximately 20% of those events should occur. Calibration groups forecasts by their assigned probability, so the events can involve different horses and races.

3. Make a forecast and score it.

Our model assigns Prior Belief 50%, Bayes Factor 30% and Posterior Favourite 20%. These are our probabilities, distinct from the price-derived reference above. How do we evaluate the whole forecast once we know the winner?

The Brier score adds up the squared differences between each horse’s predicted probability and its outcome: 1 if it won, 0 otherwise. Try changing our forecast or choosing a different winner to see how each error contributes.

We average that score across races. In this notebook we sum over all horses before averaging races, so the score is not divided by field size. With this summed, full-field definition, scores range from 0 for a perfect forecast to 2 for certainty in the wrong winner.

Brier is a proper scoring rule: in expectation, reporting the probability distribution you actually believe minimizes the score. We also use log loss, which penalizes assigning a very small probability to the eventual winner. A lower average score is evidence of better probabilistic performance, but is not, by itself, a calibration certificate. [4]

From the concepts to the system

Now, finally:
the forecasting experiment.

Our goal is to identify disagreements between the probabilities implied by betting odds and our own estimates. For each race, we produce a win-probability distribution, compare it with the market, and investigate where the difference could indicate a mispriced outcome.

01 / Form our own probabilities
Build a statistical forecast from historical records and printed form. Use a research agent to extract additional evidence, and test whether it improves those estimates.
02 / Locate disagreements
Compare each horse’s estimated chance with the raw and normalized probabilities implied by its odds. Flag cases where our model assigns a meaningfully higher or lower chance than the market.
03 / Test whether they mean anything
A disagreement is a candidate for investigation. Use later outcomes, calibration and scoring rules to assess whether our estimates are reliable and improve on the market reference. Any claim of betting value also needs executable prices and costs.

The intended system forecasts an hour before the race. Current development experiments use a deadline on the previous day, and the historical price fields are not certified closing or one-hour-before-start quotes. The market comparison therefore has explicit limits.

A small racing and forecasting glossary
Declaration
The official pre-race list of intended runners and their details. A horse may subsequently not start.
Form
A horse’s earlier performances, often printed as a compact string of placings and status codes.
Meeting
A programme of races at one venue on a particular day. Conditions can be shared across its races.
Baseline
A reference forecast against which we test an added feature or a different method.
Benchmark
A fixed collection of forecasting tasks and outcomes, with rules for comparison.
Feature
A numerical input to the model, such as a past win rate or a source-backed recommendation.
Held-out evaluation
Evaluation on data kept out of fitting and model selection. An inspected development set is not a fresh blind test.

Chapter 01

First, reconstruct
the decision before the race.

To test a forecast on an old race, we need two separate records: what the predictor could have seen beforehand, and who won afterward.

We began by collecting historical results into a database. That gave us outcomes to score against and earlier performances to learn from. But a results page is an account written after the event. Its list of horses may omit a declared horse that never started. Using that list would quietly give the forecaster knowledge of the future.

We therefore recovered the declarations: the official documents listing the intended field before each race. These became the input to the historical forecasting task. The results stayed separate, available to the evaluator after predictions were committed. A fixed collection of these tasks and their later outcomes is what we call the benchmark. [6]

Knowing the field is not enough. We also need a deadline.

Suppose we find a January preview in November. The race is old and the article carries an early date, but that does not independently prove those exact words were available before the race. We have to distinguish the event date, the publisher’s date claim and when someone actually observed or archived the text.

The historical experiments use publisher-documented timing: official document dates and supported article dates are accepted under an explicit availability assumption. A stricter test would require a verified pre-deadline observation or archive capture. That stronger proof is incomplete for this benchmark. [7]

This leaves us with a usable development playground, while limiting what it can establish about real-time historical performance. The planned one-hour-before-racing system will need prospectively timestamped inputs.

Dataset sizes, chronological splits and declared nonstarters

The master database contains 1,593 selected races, 312 meetings and 12,872 runner-result rows across eight Spanish venues, spanning November 2022–November 2026. Collection began with 12 official JCE races and expanded through Masdehipódromos. These are collected records, not certified complete national coverage.

The first H1 2026 benchmark used result-derived fields: 161 races and 1,395 runner records. The declaration-based replacement uses 33 official PDFs: 155 races and 1,404 declared entries, including 54 declared horses absent from the earlier result fields. They are different populations, not two directly comparable runner totals.

All declared horses remain in the prediction. A horse that later does not start receives zero winning target mass; it is not removed and its probability is not redistributed after the event. Snapshots preserve source URLs, hashes, observation times and provider identities.

Chapter 02

Our first attempt:
ask the agent to research and predict.

A language model seemed a natural way to use information that does not arrive in a neat table: race previews, comments about a horse and reports on its earlier performances.

Our first design gave the agent both jobs. It would ask what it needed to know, search for sources, read the admitted evidence and return a full set of win probabilities. Here, an agent means a language model working through a sequence of bounded tasks and tool calls.

The important boundary was between finding a page and being allowed to use it. A search result might be irrelevant, undated or written after the race. We added code checks for timing and source identity, followed by model review for provenance, outcome leakage and relevance. Only unanimously approved passages could enter the forecast. [8]

The first checkpoint exposed two different problems.

On 5 November, we tried races with 4, 8 and 12 declared runners, asking for a prediction both with and without research. Of the six requested forecasts, only three were valid probability distributions. Across 36 searches, no passage was admitted; many sources lacked supported dates.

That meant a score difference between the two versions could not show the benefit of new evidence: none had reached the predictor. At the same time, asking the language model to produce a complete probability distribution was unreliable. [9]

We needed to separate two questions: can the language model extract useful information? And how should that information change the probabilities?

The full research architecture and its limits

Roles share local llama3.1:8b weights. Separate conversations limit shared context, but do not make the roles independent experts. Default budgets are 12 searches, 12 pages and 64 HTTP requests per race, with at most eight admitted paragraphs and five exact sentence selections.

The predictor receives no rejected text, judge reasoning, raw search results or outcome tables. Code validates the field, finite probabilities and a sum of one. Invalid responses remain invalid; they are not silently normalized. These are code and message boundaries, not proof of operating-system isolation or freedom from model memorization.

A separate diagnostic: resolving which race a passage describes

A later preview may mention the target race as an earlier performance. An experimental event-grounded resolver tried to identify the event and temporal anchors sentence by sentence before the three review roles. Corrected small real-passage replays admitted neither useful nor leaking cases. Rejecting everything did not solve useful retrieval. This remains a separate experimental policy. [10]

Where synthetic data fits: testing the rules before trusting them

The main benchmark uses historical races. Synthetic data serves a different purpose: testing controlled behaviors in the pipeline. The engine fixture defines an invented 1,200 m race with three horses, a January 10 target and January 9 deadline. Fake adapters prescribe a source, review decisions, sentence selection and a 0.50 / 0.30 / 0.20 forecast.

Changing one input makes a failure observable. Move the source after the deadline: it must be rejected before model review. Add one veto: the passage must not reach the predictor. Return an unknown sentence ID or malformed probability distribution: it must remain rejected. Audit checks also detect altered recorded ledgers. These fixtures are not a simulated racing population for measuring forecast accuracy. [11]

Separate local-model smoke checks exercise the positive path with synthetic evidence. Four synthetic leakage probes cover earlier history, an undated target outcome, prompt injection and a late source. Passing selected cases checks implementation behavior, not general forecasting skill or population-level review sensitivity.

Chapter 03

A statistical model predicts.
The agent supplies information.

We moved probability estimation into a statistical model. The language model could then focus on reading, with a smaller and more testable output.

First we needed a reference forecast: the history model. It learns from earlier racing records, using signals such as past win rate, recent finishing strength, distance-related performance and missing history. It gives every declared horse a score, then converts all scores into a distribution summing to one.

We then added the horse’s printed form: the compact record of past performances in its declaration. A deterministic parser converts those strings into numerical summaries. No language model is needed to copy and summarize fields we already have. [12]

This gives us a natural comparison for text research: keep the existing forecast and form information, then ask whether article recommendations add anything. A feature is simply a numerical input, such as “the preview selected this horse.”

Give the added information a fair test.

The history model is fixed before these experiments. The extra adjustments for B and C learn from the same 20 January races. We then compare A, B and C on the same 28 February races. March–June is reserved for later work.

A failed article extraction must not remove an otherwise usable horse or race. Its deterministic form remains, while the additional article information is unavailable. Otherwise we would change the training population as well as the information being tested.

How the historical model was fitted

The history model fits on 799 races from 2022–2024. Regularization is selected on 343 January–September 2025 races; the model refits on those combined 1,142 races. Temperature is selected on a separate 89 October–December 2025 races. The chosen L2 penalty is 0.1 and temperature is 0.75. Temperature changes distribution sharpness; it does not establish calibration.

The six historical features are smoothed win rate, appearance count, recent finish strength, distance-weighted win rate, days since last recorded appearance and missing history. Unknown or nonfinishing positions receive fixed neutral strength 0.5. Fitting uses strictly earlier calendar days, withholding same-day results. Histories for the development predictions freeze at the end of 2025; 50 of the 368 January–February declared runners have no matched history.

Printed form uses order-independent summaries: known form, debutant status, mean reciprocal numeric placing, win fraction, code count and unknown-code fraction. Leading zeros are preserved; codes are not invented finishing positions.

The probability model and its equations

The probability target and fitting objective

Let D be the complete declared field and I(t) the information admitted under the chosen policy by cutoff t. The target is P(winner = i | D, I(t)), with i in D. It is a probability over the declaration available at prediction time, rather than a distribution retrospectively conditioned on which horses started. The benchmark retains later nonstarters with zero winning target mass.

The linear baseline minimizes mean race cross entropy plus an L2 penalty: −(1/R) ∑r log pr,winner + λ‖w‖². Standardization uses fitting data only. In the offset experiments, the baseline log probabilities stay fixed and only extra feature coefficients are learned. Softmax gives a coherent race distribution, but does not guarantee calibration, causal feature effects or historical validity of its inputs.

Article availability is itself a feature of the collection process. “Unknown” is not evidence against a horse. The observed correlation between recommendation-known and selection in January is why the later opposed-horse behavior needs a new, independently specified protocol.

Chapter 04

Can reading a preview
add useful information?

Rather than ask for an opinion about the whole race, we asked the model one narrow question about one horse: is the publisher selecting it, opposing it, or saying neither?

An earlier combined extractor had produced no usable article signal and had dropped races when extraction failed. That changed the training data as well as the features. We corrected the comparison to keep the same races, then gave article reading its own narrower test. The earlier mistake and its control are recorded in the expandable note below.

We tested this on a fixed set of preserved sources, so we could inspect extraction separately from internet search. The corpus contains 29 paragraphs from three sources, including previews for January 11 and February 1. A passage must name the horse, concern the target date and pass the publisher-documented timing rule.

The model returns selected, opposed or unknown, plus a supplied evidence ID. Code uses that ID to copy the source paragraph. The model never receives the race outcome or prices. This tests reading from controlled sources; it does not measure the success of the complete online research pipeline. [13]

The same toy horses / An invented preview

“Prior Belief was our pick last month. For Saturday’s race, we prefer Bayes Factor.”

For the target race, Bayes Factor is selected. Prior Belief is only a historical reference. Copying the quote exactly is not enough: the model must understand which horse and which race the recommendation concerns.

First check: did the model actually extract anything?

Across 368 January–February runner records, only 34 had eligible passages and required a model call. Thirty replies passed validation and four were rejected. Of the valid replies, 26 contained an actual recommendation: 20 selected and six opposed. The other four were unknown; the 334 records without eligible passages stayed unknown without inference.

That gave the statistical model some additional information, but only on a small part of the data. In a selected, separately reviewed reference set of 26 cases, the extractor got 20 classifications right. It missed compact tip lists and confused some earlier opponents or withdrawn favourites with current recommendations. These references were prepared by the coding assistant, not independently annotated by humans.

The extraction result is therefore mixed: there is a usable signal to test, but also sparse coverage and known interpretation errors. We preserve those errors and unavailable cases in the evaluation.

An earlier mistake: why we had to fix the comparison first

Before this isolated article test, an earlier combined form-and-article extractor appeared worse than deterministic form. But form was fitted on 20 January races, while failed extractions left only 16 for the agent version. Matching February evaluation races did not fix that different training population.

All accepted form vectors matched the deterministic parser, and every accepted article column was zero. A form-only refit on the same 16 races reproduced the old agent predictions to numerical precision. The comparison had not established that useful article information hurt prediction.

The corrected control kept all 20 January and all 28 February races, preserving deterministic form when optional extraction failed. With the old zero-signal article outputs, the feature forecasts became effectively identical: maximum probability difference 8.42 × 10−8. That was a control result, not the later article experiment reported below. [14]

Coverage, rejected replies and a training-support limitation

The four rejected replies declared “unknown” while still supplying an evidence ID. They were not repaired. The selected reference set missed four picks in a compact tip list, misread one historical opponent as a current selection and labelled one withdrawn former favourite opposed instead of unknown. Its 76.9% accuracy is not a population precision estimate.

January contains selected recommendations but no opposed recommendations. The opposed coefficient is therefore zero, while selected and evidence-known flags are collinear and learn equal positive coefficients. An opposed horse in February can consequently receive an uplift through the known flag. The model has not learned a reliable opposition effect.

Chapter 05

Did the extra information
improve the forecast?

This is the comparison the earlier steps were designed to make: the same races, the same basic information, and article features as the addition being tested.

Decide what “better” means before reading the table.

We use the same Brier score introduced in the three-horse example, now averaged over real races. Log loss supplies a second view: how much probability did we give the eventual winner? Both reward useful probability forecasts; lower is better.

Scores alone do not establish calibration. We also compare predicted probabilities with observed win frequencies and examine how uncertain the differences between models are.

The article forecast C has the lowest point scores. Compared with B, its Brier score decreases by about 0.00208 and its log loss by about 0.02153. This is the promising observation. The next question is whether this small development sample makes it convincing.

How much confidence should we place in that difference?

Races at one meeting—the same venue’s daily programme—share conditions. We therefore resample complete meetings when estimating uncertainty in the paired score difference. February contains only six meetings, so there is little independent variation to work with.

The result justifies further testing, not a dependable improvement claim. Extraction errors, limited sources, an unsupported opposition effect and the already-inspected development period all remain relevant.

And what about calibrated uncertainty?

The pooled probability checks look somewhat closer to observed frequencies for C, but use only 214 runners from these 28 dependent races. They describe this sample; they do not establish general calibration. The new feature models have no separately fitted calibration or per-horse uncertainty intervals. Calibration remains a research objective.

The second comparison / Forecasts versus market information

The market comparison needs its own matched sample.

Only 17 February races have complete usable reported-price fields. On those same 17 races, Brier is 0.827570 for B, 0.822971 for C and 0.799499 for the price proxy. The proxy scores better.

These are post-result reported prices, converted by the same inverse-price normalization explained at the start. They are not verified closing or one-hour-before-racing quotes. There are zero certified historical T−60 fields and zero verified exact closing fields in this evaluation. This is a limited comparator, not a completed real-time market test. Do not compare its 17-race scores directly with the 28-race table.

A difference between our probability and a price-derived probability measures disagreement. Scoring both against the same outcomes is what tests which forecast performed better.

Score formulas, uncertainty calculation and calibration diagnostics

For one winner, yi is 1 for that horse and 0 for the others. Brier sums over runners, then reported scores average races equally. It is not divided by field size.

Brierr = ∑i ∈ field (pi − yi)²
Log lossr = −log pwinner

A zero-probability winner gives infinite log loss. Coverage and invalid outputs must be reported alongside scores.

Paired intervals use 4,000 whole-meeting bootstrap draws, seed 20261112, preserving races within meetings and weighting races equally. Percentile intervals exclude model-fitting uncertainty. Descriptive ten-bin pooled-runner ECE is 0.02663 for A, 0.01823 for B and 0.01667 for C. Bins contain dependent runners and small counts.

The price proxy normalizes inverse prices under a decimal gross-return assumption: qi = (1/oddsi) / ∑j(1/oddsj). Incomplete or invalid price fields exclude entire races; runners never receive invented prices.

Chapter 06

What have we learned,
and what should happen next?

The project has become a testable chain: define what was available, extract information, convert it into probabilities, then compare forecasts against outcomes.

The first direct-agent attempt exposed both evidence-access and probability-output problems. Separating reading from estimation made those failures easier to locate. A matched comparison then made it possible to ask what article features actually added.

So far, the isolated article model improves the latest development point scores, while the price proxy remains stronger on the available matched subset. We have not established dependable research value, certified calibration or real-time market advantage.

01

Improve and independently review extraction

Handle compact tip lists and historical references correctly. Add more earlier preview dates and both positive and adverse recommendations, then measure coverage and semantic accuracy.

02

Freeze the next comparison before evaluating it

Fix extraction rules, missingness, cohorts, fitting and calibration choices before using March–June outcomes. Preserve the development failures and corrections.

03

Test a real forecasting deadline

Collect declarations, research evidence and complete prices prospectively with timestamps. Commit predictions before the deadline and evaluate only after the races.

The question remains the same:
Can we predict usefully, and know how much to trust the prediction?
